Rhythm Rush

A procedural obby that moves to the music. My first Roblox project, built in 2023–2024 and shelved before release.

Roblox · Luau · ~300 hours · I built the main game loop, the procedural obby system and RhythmGen

How it started

I saw a video of a visual polyrhythm and wondered what would happen if I put it into 3D space, so I decided to try turning it into an obby. Before there was any music detection it was called Polyrhythm Dash. The platforms just moved in that sequence and your goal was to get across. Every platform had its own piano note and played it each time it hit the end of its path, so crossing the course played the whole chromatic scale. It was very cool to me at first.

From Dash to Rhythm Rush

Then I moved on to beat detection, about 100 songs and a procedural obby generator. That part took over a week of 12–15 hour days. Every round the game picks a song and a difficulty, then builds the course from five random sections out of 22 types. Each section gets its own animation, easing style and speed, and all of it is timed off the song's BPM. Traps like land mines get welded onto the moving platforms, so they ride the rhythm too.

I got to the idea of a pendulum wave completely on my own, with no research, just my musical intuition. Mapping it to a line of platforms was the start. Mapping it to an entire 2D grid of platforms was the cherry on top. From there I tied it to different animations on different properties of the bricks (size, position, rotation) and layered visuals on top:

  • the borders on the blocks pulse directly with the song's loudness
  • an array of colors loops through each section of blocks on every detected beat
  • the beat sphere in the sky grows and shrinks with the music

All of that runs off a single loudness value, which is the only audio data Roblox gave me to work with.

Why 63/64

Syncing the animations came down to taking the BPM and running each platform in a section a tiny bit faster than the one before it:

-- each platform in a section runs slightly faster than the one before it
local newtweentime = tweeninfo.Time / ((delay_count + 63) / 64)

I tried other ratios first. Polyrhythm Dash's difficulties used 7/8, 3/4 and 1/2, and I also tried 15/16. The variation at the end of the section was just too big: the last platform always moved far too fast. 63/64 struck the right balance, where the end of a section only gets slightly harder to time.

Staying in multiples of 4 to keep it musically in sync was an educated guess I played with, and it holds up. With one beat per swing, platform k makes 63 + k swings in the time the first one makes 64, so every platform lines back up after exactly 64 round trips: 128 beats, or 32 bars of 4/4. The chaos resolves right on a phrase boundary.

The beat detector

Roblox only gives you a song's loudness on the player's own machine, so the color system runs there. It keeps the last 20 loudness readings and counts a beat when the current one is 20% above their average. It won't fire again until the loudness drops back under that line, so one long hit doesn't count as five beats. The demo above runs the same loop on whatever you play.

table.insert(loudnessHistory, loudness)
if #loudnessHistory > maxHistorySize then
	table.remove(loudnessHistory, 1)
end

local sum = 0
for _, value in ipairs(loudnessHistory) do
	sum = sum + value
end
threshold = sum / #loudnessHistory * 1.2

if loudness < threshold then
	loud_debounce = false
end
if loudness >= threshold and not loud_debounce then
	loud_debounce = true
	-- step every platform one color forward
end

Why it never shipped

Rog hopped on in the middle of development. I posted about the project in a Discord, he DM'd me, and that's how we started working together. We've worked as a duo ever since: Rog handles data, security and gameplay balance, and I handle game design and creative engineering (map building, visual scripting and overall direction). On Rhythm Rush my part was the main game loop, the procedural obby system and RhythmGen.

We never published it because we couldn't figure out a good progression loop for players to race, earn and spend, so we shelved it and moved on to other projects. I'm still incredibly proud of it. I had just gotten back into programming for the first time since I was a kid, and I'd picked up music production as a hobby, so I wanted to do something ambitious that used both.

What I'd do differently

  • Lock the motion to the song's playback position. Polyrhythm Dash scheduled every loop from the start time and stayed in sync. Rhythm Rush replays each tween when it finishes, so it slips about a frame per loop and the 32-bar line-up doesn't land exactly.
  • Measure the detector's window in time, not frames, so it behaves the same at 60 fps and 144 fps.
  • Describe the 22 section types as data in one table instead of 22 nearly identical functions.
  • Design the progression loop before the tech. That's what actually stopped it.
